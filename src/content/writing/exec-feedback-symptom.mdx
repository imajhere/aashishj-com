I once spent three rounds of review trying to get a senior leader to sign off on a template system — a set of documents my team would use to run spec-driven work. Each round, the feedback was some version of "this still feels off." Never a crisp requirement. Never "change X to Y." Just a reaction.

The tempting move is to treat that as a guessing game: keep proposing variations until one stops drawing a frown. That way lies infinite rounds. The better move is to treat the reaction as a *symptom* and go find what it's pointing at.

## Reaction, not requirement

Senior feedback is very often a felt sense that something's wrong, not a specification for how to fix it. That's not a failure on the leader's part — noticing that something's off is a different, faster skill than articulating why. But it does relocate the work. **The job isn't to extract a spec they don't have. It's to convert their reaction into a concrete, defensible decision they can recognize as right.**

So after each round I stopped asking "what do you want changed?" and instead synthesized the reaction into a specific structural move, then brought that back. Round by round, the system got *smaller.* I dropped a framing that was creating confusion. I treated the whole set of documents as a single approval unit instead of a pile of separate ones. And ultimately I consolidated four templates into three — folding two overlapping specs into a single document.

## Naming was half the problem

One of the most useful changes wasn't structural at all. The word "spec" was doing too much work — it appeared across five different artifacts, meaning something slightly different each time. No amount of restructuring could fix the confusion, because the confusion was *lexical.* Renaming one central document so an outsider could tell it apart from the others did more for clarity than any content edit. When a term is overloaded, people feel the friction long before they can name it — and "this feels off" is exactly what that friction sounds like.

## The pattern

Two things I now reach for whenever feedback is vague and senior.

First: **when you can't get a requirement, propose a decision.** Turn the reaction into a specific, defensible choice and let them react to *that.* A concrete option is far easier to confirm or reject than an open question is to answer.

Second: **the winning move is usually to cut, not add.** Every round of this shrank the system — fewer templates, fewer headings, clearer names — and every round it got *better.* Vague discomfort with something complex is very often complexity itself asking to be reduced. When a leader says "this feels off," simplification is a shockingly good first guess.
