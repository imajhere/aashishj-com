I kept having the same argument with my AI assistant. Its answers were technically correct and genuinely exhausting to read — dense, jargon-heavy, no clear takeaway. So every few messages I'd add "keep it simple" or "just give me the point," get one good reply, and then watch it drift right back to a wall of text in the next conversation.

At some point I noticed the pattern in *my own* behavior: I was giving the same feedback over and over. And repeated feedback isn't a preference you express — it's a **rule you haven't written down yet.**

## Encode it once

So I sat down and wrote the assistant an actual rule for *how* it should talk to me. Not what to do — how to say it. Plain language over jargon. A warm tone. Breathing room instead of dense blocks. Examples and analogies where they help something land. Visuals — a table, a few steps, a small diagram — but only when they genuinely cut the effort of reading, never as decoration.

The important refinement was tailoring by *type* of response. A status update should be terse. Something I'm trying to learn should be richer, with the analogy and the example. One rule, but context-aware, so it isn't uniformly verbose or uniformly clipped.

Since then I don't re-explain. The preference lives in one place and applies every session. The recurring friction just… stopped.

## A wrinkle worth knowing

If you go to do this, know that these tools tend to store your rules in **two different places,** and they behave differently. Some live in an editable rule file the assistant can read and even revise for you. Others are locked in a settings block inside the app's own storage — which means the assistant *can't* edit those; you have to paste them in yourself. Worth knowing before you wonder why your carefully-worded instruction didn't take.

## "Short" is not "clear"

The subtlest thing I learned came from a collision between my new rule and an older one I'd written that just said *be as short as possible.* Those two rules fought. The style rule wanted room to breathe and an occasional example; the old rule wanted the fewest possible words.

The resolution reframed what I actually wanted. The real target was never fewest *words* — it was **lowest effort to understand.** Sometimes that means fewer words. But sometimes a one-line example or a small table makes something click instantly that a terse sentence would have made me re-read three times. Optimizing for word count can actively make an answer *worse.* "Short" and "clear" point in the same direction often enough to feel identical — and then, right when it matters, they don't.

So: if you keep telling your AI the same thing, stop telling it and write it a rule. And when you do, aim the rule at clarity, not brevity. They're not the same goal, and only one of them is the one you actually care about.
